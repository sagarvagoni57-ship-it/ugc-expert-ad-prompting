# Reference patterns from the supplied UGC library

## Scope and how to use this file

The supplied library contains 57 standalone reference bundles: 20 HyperMotion-style UGC examples and 37 TikTok/UGC examples. The bundles contain 1-fps frame samples, factual visual notes and either recognized dialogue or an explicit no-dialogue note. They are creative references, not proof that a format converts, a claim is true, or a brand has authorized reuse.

Use the library to select a **structure**, then rebuild the structure around the user's product, facts and audience. Do not copy the reference video's product, actor identity, exact words, logo, distinctive character, or protected visual identity. If a supplied reference is used, mention which elements were borrowed: hook, blocking, pacing, camera perspective or sound idea.

## Product-to-archetype map

### 1. Direct-to-camera product demonstration

Use for gadgets, beauty tools, food, supplements where claims are available, accessories and simple products with one visible feature. The creator looks at the camera, holds or operates the product, and names one concrete thing the viewer can understand.

Observed pattern in the library: the product is visible immediately, the creator uses short spoken lines, then a close-up or operation proves the line. Examples include the robot demo, blender tutorial, lollipop reveal, protein-powder presentation and gadget demonstrations.

Prompt decisions:

- Start with the creator already holding or operating the product.
- Name one feature before adding enthusiasm.
- Show the exact action that makes the feature legible.
- Use a small reaction tied to the action, not an uninterrupted smile.
- End with a product hold and one low-pressure CTA.

### 2. Problem-to-method tutorial

Use when the product has a safe, observable method: a blender, hair-care tool, organizer, cleaning item, kitchen tool or software-like gadget. The ad shows a recognizable mistake or friction, then a short correct method.

Observed pattern: a hook names a familiar problem; the creator shows the incorrect or inefficient action briefly; the product action follows; the final frame shows the practical state. The supplied hair routine, blender and mess-to-fresh references follow this rhythm.

Prompt decisions:

- Keep the problem non-shaming and visually simple.
- Show only the amount of incorrect behavior needed to establish context.
- Demonstrate one supported method; do not invent a dosage or guarantee a result.
- Make the payoff visible as organization, texture, sound, movement or completion—not as a medical transformation.

### 3. Unboxing and first reveal

Use when packaging, arrival, material, assortment or first physical interaction is itself useful. The supplied Delivery, Unboxing, Unboxing ASMR, Mystery Box, Reboxing and product-reveal examples use this structure.

Prompt decisions:

- Establish the package and environment in the first shot.
- Give opening, removal and presentation separate beats; do not demand all three in one gesture.
- Let the hand finish one action before cutting to a detail insert.
- If a taste, wear or use step is shown, make sure it is appropriate and supported.
- If the real product has unknown packaging, do not force an unboxing; use a product-hold demo instead.

### 4. Try-on, styling or transition

Use for clothing, shoes, eyewear, wearable accessories and appearance-led products. The library includes outfit try-ons, sneaker styling and gym-set transitions.

Prompt decisions:

- Use an intentional before state and after state: same actor, stable camera, clear cut.
- Name only visible fit, color, material or styling facts that the reference supports.
- Show one functional movement such as a walk, squat, turn, lace tie or mirror check.
- Avoid "fits everyone", exact sizing, comfort or performance claims unless supplied.
- Keep wardrobe and body continuity explicit; do not add a new actor between shots.

### 5. Selfie review-style explanation

Use when a conversational creator can explain a product choice or feature without pretending to be a real customer. The Review, Selfie-Testimonial and Direct-to-Camera references use a face-forward presentation.

Prompt decisions:

- Use a natural opening such as a question, observation or demonstration cue.
- Phrase evidence as what is visible or what the product is designed to do: "The label shows...", "This control lets you...", "Here is how it opens...".
- Do not write fabricated purchase history, weeks-of-use claims, cure claims, review counts or "game changer" language unless the user provides substantiated evidence and wants that exact claim checked.
- Keep one direct eye-line beat and one product-detail beat.

### 6. ASMR and tactile reveal

Use for food, packaging, stationery, beauty jars, accessories and products where sound or texture helps the viewer understand the object. The supplied Unboxing ASMR and other close-detail references use tapping, sliding, opening, pouring or a bite.

Prompt decisions:

- Decide whether the ad is spoken, whisper-led or sound-first before writing the prompt.
- List the intended sounds: paper slide, lid click, zipper, pour, tap, bite or fabric movement.
- Keep the microphone perspective plausible and leave clean pauses.
- If the model cannot reliably produce sound, mark the sound design for post and keep the visual action complete.

### 7. Couple, friend or family share

Use when sharing is intrinsic to the product: snacks, games, home products or gifts. The supplied couple-at-home references use one person introducing the product, a second person trying it, then a shared payoff.

Prompt decisions:

- Cast only the second person needed for the interaction.
- Give each person a simple action and clear eye line.
- Do not add a second person just to make the ad feel cinematic.
- The interaction can show sharing or reaction, but it must not become fabricated social proof.

### 8. Camera POV and playful pet interruption

Use for pet products, snacks and products where a point-of-view joke clarifies desirability. The supplied Camera-POV references use security-camera/fisheye framing and an animal approaching an unattended product.

Prompt decisions:

- State the camera source clearly: fixed security camera, phone POV or handheld. Do not combine all three.
- Keep the animal behavior safe, physically plausible and supervised.
- Do not let a pet consume a product unless that use is explicitly safe and supplied.
- Use the joke to reveal the product, then finish with a human/product frame.

### 9. Comparison, scale or spectacle

Use sparingly for collectibles, lamps, novelty products or a product whose size/variant comparison is the actual idea. The supplied Giant Figure and Classic-Meets-Modern examples show that scale or a visually unusual world can stop the scroll.

Prompt decisions:

- Explain why the spectacle helps the viewer understand the product.
- Preserve product identity first; the spectacular background is secondary.
- Keep one simple comparison action: hold, step back, point, clip, light or rotate.
- Do not default to giant props, historical scenes, floating products or destructive tests for ordinary products.

## Recurring visual lessons

Across the supplied frames, the strongest reusable patterns are:

1. Product or product context appears in the opening beat rather than after a long introduction.
2. A short hook is paired with an immediate action: hold, open, pour, wear, press, taste, compare or reach.
3. The camera alternates between a human context shot and a readable product/detail shot.
4. Most shots have one dominant action; fast pacing comes from clean cuts, not impossible multi-step choreography.
5. Hands, product scale, wardrobe and background are kept consistent enough that the viewer can understand what changed.
6. Dialogue is conversational and specific; the most useful lines name a feature, method, option, texture, style or next step.
7. The closing frame leaves the product readable and gives one simple action such as "See the details", "Save this idea" or "Explore the product".
8. Many source clips have no intelligible dialogue or no audio stream. A prompt must therefore specify whether speech is essential and provide a post-production fallback.
9. The supplied clips vary in duration and aspect ratio. Use their pacing as inspiration, but write the user's requested 20-second edit map explicitly.

## What the agent must not learn as a rule

- Do not copy exaggerated reactions merely because a reference has them.
- Do not turn claims from a source creative into verified product facts.
- Do not force a before/after, testimonial, unboxing, giant figure or pet joke onto a product that does not support it.
- Do not use extreme realism keyword piles as a substitute for a coherent camera, light, location and action plan.
- Do not treat 1-fps frame samples as a production storyboard with exact cut boundaries; they are evidence for visual analysis.
- Do not imply that a supplied reference is a brand-approved template or that its performance has been measured.
